The Onsen That Chose
Not to Be a Spa

In March 2026, a small tōji bathhouse in the hills above Hakone posted a notice: no banquets, no tour groups, no parties of five or more. The guests it wants are the ones who need to rest.

By Takayama Sauna, Editor-in-Chief · Sauna producer, supervisor of award-winning facilities · 4 min read · July 2026

On March 1, 2026, a small day-use bathhouse in the hills above Hakone posted a notice on its own website. The headline reads, in Japanese: "We are not a day-trip hot spring facility." What follows is a list of what the house will no longer host: meetings, seminars, tour groups, banquets, corporate entertaining, any party of five or more. It closes with the guests it does want: people caring for family, people worn down by work, people recovering from illness or injury.

That bathhouse is Shumeikan, at Ubako Onsen — described as the last tōji bathhouse still operating at a spring that has drawn visitors since the Edo period. Its own words: "a small tōji bathhouse that people visit for the sake of resting body and mind" (心身の療養を目的とした方々が通われる小さな湯治場です). While Japan's bathing boom scales up reservation-driven leisure, Shumeikan is enforcing an older definition of what a bath visit is for.

What tōji actually is

Tōji (湯治) is hot-spring bathing taken for recuperation. Historically, farmers and travelers spent the agricultural off-season at a spring — bathing repeatedly, resting, eating simply. Shumeikan offers no overnight stay, so it compresses that rhythm into visits of two hours to a full day, under its own name for the practice: kayoi tōji, "commute-style tōji."

The house sells a bathing seat and a block of time, not a service menu. Most guests reserve a yudoko, a resting spot for one or two people, in two-hour, four-hour, or full-day blocks from the 10 a.m. reception window. A yudoko is not a private room: bathing happens in one small shared bath each for men and women. The few larger tatami rooms go mainly to families bringing someone who needs help bathing.

The rest of the operation follows the same logic. No advance bookings — admission is same-day, first-come. Soap and shampoo are banned in the baths, a rule the facility attributes to protecting the spring. Quiet is policy, not atmosphere: the house bars anything that would disturb another guest's rest.

Water that comes when it rains

The spring has an unusual property: it doesn't always flow.

Ubako's water rises from rock exposed when the flank of Mt. Kamiyama collapsed roughly 3,000 years ago, according to the facility and local tourism sources. The spring is a natural upwelling, not a drilled well, and its output tracks rainfall — it typically surfaces around three times a year, with the spring rains, the rainy season, and the autumn rains. When it isn't flowing, the facility switches to a reserve well on site.

The dependency shows up in Shumeikan's news feed like a weather report. On June 29, 2026, the site posted a notice titled "The water is here." This year's natural flow had arrived.

Local lore adds a story: the folk hero Kintarō is said to have healed an injured eye in this water as a child — a widely repeated legend, not a documented event. Better documented is the long use. Records connect bathing at Ubako to the Edo period, and the facility is generally described as a Meiji-to-Taishō-era establishment, though we haven't independently verified a founding year.

If you go

Go on the facility's terms. There is no reservation system — arrive early for a full-day slot, since the 10–11 a.m. window fills first-come. As of a July 17, 2026 check against Shumeikan's own site, a two-hour yudoko booking — bathing fee, robe, and towel included, not a private room — runs ¥2,000 per person, and a full day of tōji is ¥5,600. Shumeikan is closed every Thursday and all of February. Car navigation often won't register "Shumeikan" as a search term — the facility asks visitors to search "Ubako Onsen" instead.

A few house rules are worth flagging, especially against the tattoo-friendly venues profiled elsewhere in this journal (Tattoo-Friendly Baths, Mapped). Shumeikan does not admit guests with tattoos, full stop — its policy states this isn't a matter of size, count, or visibility. (Tenzan, a separate facility under the same operator, currently allows tattoos for solo guests only — the two shouldn't be confused.) Children three and under aren't admitted, the facility serves no meals, outside alcohol isn't allowed in, and photography and video are banned. Groups of friends are also asked, as house preference rather than a rule, to keep visits small.

Operational details come from Shumeikan's own website, checked on the dates cited; historical context draws on local tourism sources. This piece doesn't include a facility visit, and terms can change — check the official site before you go. For tōji built into a longer itinerary, that's what our Bespoke service is for.
