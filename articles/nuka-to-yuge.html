Off-map · Kyotango

The Bath With
No Water

In Kyotango, a mound of fermenting rice bran does the job hot water usually does: you are buried to the chin in warm, live bran, then shower it off. Japan's fermentation culture, turned into a bath.

By Takayama Sauna, Editor-in-Chief · Sauna producer, supervisor of award-winning facilities · 3 min read · July 2026

There is no faucet in this room, no showerhead, no pool. What waits is a long wooden trough of pale, warm rice bran. A staff member buries you in it, hands to chin, only your face above the surface. No heater, either: the mound generates its own heat, one microbe generation at a time, the way a compost pile warms up in the corner of a garden.

This is a kōso-buro, an enzyme bath. The facility that runs it — Nuka to Yuge, "bran and steam," in Kyotango on the Sea of Japan coast of northern Kyoto Prefecture — pairs it with five private Finnish-style saunas, a combination the operator claims as a first. Almost nothing about the place exists in English, which is why this piece does. It is built from the facility's published materials and independent reporting, not a first-person soak.

Fermentation as a heat source

The bran comes from Kyotango-area farms, according to the facility. It is the same raw material as nukazuke, the rice-bran pickles found in Japanese kitchens for centuries — but the bath is not slow pickling. Nukazuke ferments through lactic-acid bacteria at room temperature and barely warms at all. The bath runs on a faster, aerobic fermentation, closer to an active compost pile, that gets genuinely hot as the microbes work through the bran.

A visit follows a fixed structure, per the facility's FAQ: about ten minutes of counseling, twenty to thirty minutes buried in the bran, twenty minutes to rinse off. Call it an hour, plus whatever you book next door in the sauna.

The founder, an orthopedic surgeon by training, opened the facility in 2022 and has run a small in-house study on the bath's effects on circulation, sleep, and pain, through a clinic connected to him. As far as we could confirm, the study has not been independently peer-reviewed. Treat the reported benefits — better circulation, warmer extremities, softer skin — as the enzyme-bath industry's standard pitch, not settled medicine.

Getting to Kyotango

Kyotango sits at the far northern edge of Kyoto Prefecture, on the coastline the tourism board brands "Kyoto by the Sea" — a transfer or two by rail from central Kyoto, or a highway drive. It is far from the temples most visitors picture when they hear the prefecture's name.

The case for the trip is the pairing. The five saunas are private, reservation-only rooms where guests pour their own löyly (water over the stove's stones), one party at a time. The private format also lets the facility commit to something many famous sauna houses won't: its FAQ (nuka-yuge.com/qanda) states plainly that tattooed guests are welcome in all five rooms — one more confirmed entry for our guide Tattoo-Friendly Baths, Mapped. The region is also one of Kyoto Prefecture's better-regarded seafood and produce areas.

If you go

Bath and sauna sell together as a set plan: ¥18,000 for about two and a half hours (with a ninety-minute private sauna) or ¥20,000 for about three and a half hours, per person, tax included — confirmed against the facility's set-plan page, nuka-yuge.com/nukayugeset, 17 July 2026. Book about three days ahead via the official LINE account or phone; same-day slots open when there is availability. Cash, card, and QR are accepted. No restaurant on site — eat nearby. Operating hours weren't posted on the official site as of this writing, so confirm times when you book.
